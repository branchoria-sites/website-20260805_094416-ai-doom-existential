## Introduction

Could AI-enabled [cyberattacks]({{ 'defence-failure/' | relative_url }}) make [nuclear crises]({{ 'nuclear-crises/' | relative_url }}) more dangerous? Potentially yes, but not because hackers are likely to seize direct control of nuclear weapons. The greater concern is that increasingly capable AI could make cyber operations faster, more scalable and more deceptive, increasing the risk that a state loses confidence in its nuclear command, control and communications (NC3) during a crisis. If leaders believe that communications, early-warning systems or retaliatory forces have already been compromised, they may feel pressure to act before those capabilities disappear. That dynamic—not the cyberattack itself—is why this topic matters within debates about AI doom and existential risk.

This risk sits within the broader question of whether AI could amplify [catastrophic]({{ 'catastrophic-misuse/' | relative_url }}) human mistakes. Most researchers do not argue that AI-driven cyberattacks make nuclear war inevitable. Instead, they argue that AI may strengthen existing escalation pathways by making it [harder to]({{ 'hidden-evals/' | relative_url }}) distinguish genuine attacks from false alarms, by reducing confidence in critical systems, and by compressing the time available for careful judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## Why nuclear command networks are strategic targets

Nuclear deterrence depends on more than missiles. It also depends on confidence that national leaders can communicate with military forces, receive accurate information, authenticate orders and maintain the ability to retaliate after absorbing an attack. These functions are collectively known as nuclear command, control and communications (NC3).

An adversary does not necessarily need to destroy an entire NC3 system to create strategic instability. Merely creating uncertainty about whether communications remain trustworthy can change leaders' calculations. During a conventional conflict, cyber operations against command networks, satellites, communications infrastructure or intelligence systems may be interpreted as preparation for a wider attempt to disable a country's nuclear deterrent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2021/04/china-us-cyber-nuclear-c3-stability?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carnegieendowment.org">[Carnegie Endowment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carnegieendowment.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carnegie EndowmentChina-U.S. Cyber-Nuclear C3 Stability &#124; Carnegie Endowment for International PeaceApril 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2021</span></span></span>

## How AI could strengthen both cyber offence and cyber defence

The relationship between AI and cyber security is not one-sided. The same technologies that may improve offensive operations can also improve defence.

### The offensive case

Cybersecurity researchers increasingly expect AI to accelerate parts of the attack cycle by assisting with vulnerability discovery, malware adaptation, reconnaissance and [automated]({{ 'full-research-loop/' | relative_url }}) analysis of large technical environments. Rather than inventing entirely new forms of cyberattack, AI mainly reduces the cost and speed of existing techniques while allowing attackers to conduct more operations simultaneously.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07228" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

Applied to military competition, this raises concern because NC3 systems are among the highest-value targets in any confrontation between nuclear-armed states.

### The defensive case

The same AI capabilities can strengthen defenders by:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* detecting unusual network behaviour more rapidly;
* prioritising alerts from huge volumes of security data;
* identifying malware patterns that humans might miss;
* automating parts of incident response;
* improving resilience through continuous monitoring.

</div>

Some analysts therefore argue that AI may ultimately improve the security of nuclear-related networks if deployed carefully under strong human oversight. Better detection and faster recovery could reduce opportunities for successful cyber intrusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.chathamhouse.org/publications/the-world-today/2025-06/what-happens-if-ai-goes-nuclear" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: chathamhouse.org">[Chatham House]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">chathamhouse.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on chathamhouse.org.</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that neither attackers nor defenders know in advance which side will gain the larger advantage during a crisis. That uncertainty itself can influence strategic behaviour.

## Why fear of disruption can encourage early escalation

One of the most important ideas in nuclear strategy is that perceptions matter almost as much as technical reality.

Imagine that one country's leadership receives evidence suggesting its military communications have been infiltrated while unusual activity is simultaneously detected in satellite networks and missile-warning systems. Even if none of these events proves decisive on its own, leaders may begin to fear that an adversary is attempting to blind them before launching a larger attack.

Researchers sometimes describe this as a "use it before you lose it" pressure. If decision-makers believe their ability to retaliate is rapidly disappearing, waiting for additional confirmation may appear increasingly [dangerous]({{ 'dangerous-autonomy/' | relative_url }}). The cyberattack does not need to eliminate the deterrent completely. It only needs to reduce confidence that the deterrent will remain available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2021/04/china-us-cyber-nuclear-c3-stability?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carnegieendowment.org">[Carnegie Endowment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carnegieendowment.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carnegie EndowmentChina-U.S. Cyber-Nuclear C3 Stability &#124; Carnegie Endowment for International PeaceApril 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2021</span></span></span>

AI could intensify this dynamic in several ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* automated attacks may spread faster than traditional cyber operations;
* AI-assisted deception may produce more convincing fake evidence;
* defensive systems may generate overwhelming volumes of alerts;
* commanders may struggle to distinguish genuine compromise from AI-generated noise.

</div>

The result is not automatic escalation but greater uncertainty during the period when accurate judgement matters most.

## False confidence can be as dangerous as real compromise

An important misconception is that only successful cyberattacks create danger.

In reality, false beliefs may be equally destabilising.

Leaders could mistakenly conclude that communications have been compromised when they have not. Conversely, they might trust AI-assisted defensive assessments that incorrectly report systems as secure. Both errors affect decision-making because nuclear crises unfold under severe time pressure and incomplete information.

Researchers studying cyber-nuclear interactions emphasise that attribution remains difficult. States may struggle to determine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* who conducted an intrusion;
* whether it is espionage or preparation for sabotage;
* whether malware has actually damaged critical systems;
* whether apparently independent failures share a common cause.

</div>

AI does not remove these uncertainties. In some circumstances it may increase them by producing larger volumes of ambiguous technical evidence that requires rapid interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2021/04/china-us-cyber-nuclear-c3-stability?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: carnegieendowment.org">[carnegieendowment.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">carnegieendowment.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Carnegie EndowmentChina-U.S. Cyber-Nuclear C3 Stability &#124; Carnegie Endowment for International PeaceApril 8, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 8, 2021</span></span></span>

## Why this matters for AI doom debates

Within discussions of [AI doom and]({{ 'ai-doom-and-existential-risk-from/' | relative_url }}) existential risk, AI-enabled cyber threats to nuclear command systems represent an indirect pathway rather than a story about AI independently deciding to launch nuclear weapons.

The proposed chain looks roughly like this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Advanced AI substantially improves cyber capabilities.
2. States increasingly target military communications and supporting infrastructure.
3. During a crisis, leaders lose confidence in the integrity of their NC3 systems.
4. Reduced confidence shortens decision time and increases incentives to escalate before capabilities appear to disappear.
5. Human decisions—not autonomous AI—produce a catastrophic outcome.

</div>

Supporters of this concern argue that AI compounds an already dangerous strategic environment by accelerating offensive cyber operations and increasing uncertainty. Critics respond that modern nuclear forces are deliberately designed with redundancy, authentication procedures and multiple communication pathways that make catastrophic failure considerably harder than simplified scenarios suggest. They also note that governments recognise the extraordinary importance of NC3 security and invest heavily in protecting it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

The evidence therefore does not show that AI makes nuclear cyber catastrophe inevitable. Rather, it suggests that AI could amplify one of the most sensitive parts of nuclear deterrence: confidence that leaders can still communicate, understand events accurately and retain credible second-strike capability under attack.

## What researchers see as the most promising risk-reduction measures

Because the greatest danger comes from uncertainty rather than AI acting independently, many proposed safeguards focus on preserving confidence and extending decision time instead of pursuing complete technological superiority.

Common recommendations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* strengthening cyber resilience around NC3 and closely related conventional systems;
* maintaining meaningful human judgement over nuclear decisions, even when AI assists analysis;
* separating nuclear command functions from less secure digital systems wherever practical;
* improving transparency and confidence-building measures between nuclear-armed states regarding AI integration;
* conducting rigorous testing of AI-assisted cyber defence before deployment in high-consequence environments;
* developing communication channels that remain available even during severe cyber disruption.

</div>

Many analysts also argue that AI governance and cyber security should not be treated as separate policy areas. As AI becomes more capable, protecting the systems that underpin nuclear deterrence increasingly depends on understanding how machine learning changes both attack and defence. The central question is therefore not whether AI can "hack the nuclear button", but whether it can erode the confidence and communication on which stable nuclear deterrence depends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[sipri.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

      var rule = timezoneRules[i] || {};
      try {

      } catch (err) {}
