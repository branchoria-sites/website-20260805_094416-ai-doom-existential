## Introduction

Could AI push a nuclear crisis too far? Possibly—but not because it invents nuclear weapons or independently launches them. The main concern is that increasingly capable AI systems could change how military leaders gather [intelligence]({{ 'hard-bottlenecks/' | relative_url }}), interpret events, make decisions and manage crises. During a confrontation between nuclear-armed states, even small increases in speed, complexity or misunderstanding can matter. If AI encourages overconfidence, compresses decision times, produces convincing but incorrect assessments, or becomes vulnerable to cyber manipulation, it could make an already [dangerous]({{ 'dangerous-autonomy/' | relative_url }}) situation harder for humans to control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

Within debates about [AI doom and]({{ 'ai-doom-and-existential-risk-from/' | relative_url }}) existential risk, this pathway sits under [catastrophic]({{ 'catastrophic-misuse/' | relative_url }}) human misuse rather than AI takeover. The worry is that people or governments might use increasingly capable AI in ways that unintentionally increase the chance of nuclear war. Most researchers do not argue that AI alone is likely to trigger a nuclear exchange. Instead, they argue that AI could amplify familiar escalation risks at moments when mistakes are least forgivable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2024/sipri-background-papers/nuclear-weapons-and-artificial-intelligence-technological-promises-and-practical-realities" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Weapons and Artificial Intelligence: Technological Promises and Practical Realities &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## Where AI could enter nuclear decision-making

Modern nuclear deterrence already depends on large, complicated systems. Satellites, radar networks, intelligence analysis, communications systems and military planning all contribute to decisions made under severe uncertainty. AI is increasingly being explored across these areas because it can process enormous volumes of information far faster than humans.

Potential applications include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* analysing satellite imagery and sensor data for possible missile launches;
* detecting unusual military movements from intelligence feeds;
* supporting cyber defence for nuclear command and control networks;
* helping commanders prioritise information during crises;
* assisting conventional military planning that indirectly affects nuclear forces.

</div>

Importantly, these uses often sit outside the nuclear launch process itself. Even so, they can still influence escalation by shaping what leaders believe is happening and how much time they think they have to respond. SIPRI argues that military AI can affect nuclear escalation even when it is deployed only in conventional systems because those systems influence perceptions, signalling and crisis management.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

Most nuclear-armed states have publicly committed to retaining human involvement in decisions about nuclear weapon use. However, exactly what "human control" means varies considerably, and there is much less agreement about how much AI should support earlier stages of decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/other-publications/pragmatic-approaches-governance-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-nexus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pragmatic Approaches to Governance at the Artificial Intelligence–Nuclear Nexus &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## How speed and uncertainty can drive escalation

Nuclear crises are dangerous because leaders often have incomplete information while facing enormous pressure to act quickly. AI could worsen several existing problems simultaneously.

### Faster decisions leave less time to question assumptions

Military AI is often promoted because it shortens the time between detecting a threat and responding to it. In many contexts that is valuable. During a nuclear confrontation, however, speed can remove opportunities for checking whether alarming information is actually correct.

History contains several near misses in which human operators identified false alarms before escalation occurred. Future systems that encourage ever-faster responses could reduce opportunities for similar human judgement. Researchers therefore worry about "compression" of decision time rather than AI replacing leaders outright.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

### AI recommendations may appear more objective than they are

Advanced AI systems often generate confident-looking outputs despite uncertainty. Military commanders under pressure may give excessive weight to algorithmic recommendations, a phenomenon sometimes called [automation bias]({{ 'automation-bias/' | relative_url }}).

This does not require AI to be perfect. The danger is that uncertain predictions may appear more precise than they really are, especially when accompanied by sophisticated explanations or probability estimates. An opaque recommendation could subtly push decision-makers towards escalation even if the underlying evidence is weak.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/2025-06/2025_6_ai_and_nuclear_risk.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">on Nuclear Escalation RiskJune 12, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 12, 2025</span></span></span>

### Multiple AI systems could interact unpredictably

Future crises may involve several countries using AI-assisted intelligence, cyber defence, surveillance and operational planning simultaneously. Each system may respond to the behaviour of the others.

Small misunderstandings could therefore cascade:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* one AI incorrectly classifies military activity as preparation for attack;
* another nation's system interprets defensive mobilisation as offensive intent;
* commanders on both sides conclude that delay increases their vulnerability;
* each side escalates while believing it is acting defensively.

</div>

This kind of feedback loop concerns many strategic analysts because deterrence already depends heavily on interpreting an opponent's intentions correctly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## AI could also increase cyber risks

Another concern is not AI advising leaders directly, but AI strengthening cyber operations against nuclear-related infrastructure.

Future AI systems could improve vulnerability discovery, automate aspects of cyber intrusion or help defenders identify attacks more quickly. If offensive capabilities advance faster than defensive ones, countries may fear that their nuclear command, control and communications systems—often abbreviated as NC3—could be disrupted during conflict.

That fear alone can influence behaviour. If leaders believe their ability to retaliate is becoming unreliable, they may feel pressure to act earlier than they otherwise would. Strategic stability depends partly on confidence that nuclear forces remain survivable even under attack. AI-enabled cyber capabilities could undermine that confidence even without destroying any weapons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2024/sipri-background-papers/nuclear-weapons-and-artificial-intelligence-technological-promises-and-practical-realities" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Weapons and Artificial Intelligence: Technological Promises and Practical Realities &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## What do simulations tell us?

Researchers have begun testing frontier language models in simplified diplomatic and military simulations. Several studies found that current models sometimes adopted surprisingly escalatory behaviour, including threats or simulated nuclear use, even when peaceful alternatives existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03408" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Escalation Risks from Language Models in Military and Diplomatic Decision-MakingJanuary 7, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 7, 2024</span></span></span>

These findings deserve attention but also careful interpretation.

The simulations are not realistic predictions of government behaviour. Today's language models are not integrated into real nuclear command systems, do not possess classified intelligence and do not experience the [institutional]({{ 'resilience/' | relative_url }}) constraints facing actual political leaders. Military organisations also involve extensive chains of command, legal oversight and consultation that are difficult to model.

Instead, these experiments are useful because they reveal potential behavioural tendencies that deserve further testing before AI is trusted in high-stakes strategic settings. They highlight the importance of evaluating systems under [adversarial]({{ 'concealment/' | relative_url }}), uncertain and time-pressured conditions rather than assuming good performance on ordinary benchmarks will translate into safe crisis behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03408" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Escalation Risks from Language Models in Military and Diplomatic Decision-MakingJanuary 7, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 7, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why human control and secure systems still matter

Many proposed safeguards focus less on banning AI outright than on limiting where it is used and ensuring humans remain genuinely responsible for irreversible decisions.

Common proposals include:

* maintaining meaningful human authority over any decision involving nuclear weapon use;
* preventing full automation of launch decisions;
* requiring independent verification of AI-generated intelligence;
* designing AI systems that clearly communicate uncertainty instead of presenting unwarranted confidence;
* strengthening cyber security around nuclear command and control systems;
* increasing transparency and confidence-building measures between nuclear-armed states regarding military AI deployments;
* developing [international]({{ 'shared-testing/' | relative_url }}) norms governing AI applications that could affect strategic stability.

These measures aim to preserve the time and judgement needed to detect mistakes before they become irreversible. SIPRI and other researchers argue that governance should address not only nuclear launch systems themselves but also AI used throughout the wider military ecosystem that influences crisis decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/other-publications/pragmatic-approaches-governance-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-nexus" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pragmatic Approaches to Governance at the Artificial Intelligence–Nuclear Nexus &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

## How this fits into AI doom debates

For people concerned about AI doom or existential risk, AI-driven nuclear escalation is usually viewed as an indirect pathway rather than the central scenario.

Unlike loss-of-control arguments—in which advanced AI itself becomes difficult to control—this risk depends on human institutions deploying increasingly capable AI into already dangerous geopolitical situations. The key question is whether AI meaningfully increases the probability of accidental, mistaken or unnecessarily rapid escalation between nuclear powers.

Reasonable experts disagree about how large that increase might be. Some argue that carefully designed AI could eventually improve situational awareness, reduce human error and strengthen crisis management. Others believe that introducing opaque, cyber-vulnerable systems into environments defined by uncertainty and extreme time pressure creates new failure modes that are difficult to predict in advance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2024/sipri-background-papers/nuclear-weapons-and-artificial-intelligence-technological-promises-and-practical-realities" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Weapons and Artificial Intelligence: Technological Promises and Practical Realities &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>

The current evidence does not support claims that AI is likely to start a nuclear war by itself. It does, however, support taking seriously the possibility that advanced AI could alter incentives, compress decision-making and complicate crisis management in ways that increase the danger of catastrophic human mistakes. That possibility is one reason the intersection of AI safety, military strategy and nuclear risk has become an increasingly important area of research and international policy discussion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sipri.org/publications/2025/sipri-insights-peace-and-security/impact-military-artificial-intelligence-nuclear-escalation-risk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sipri.org">[SIPRI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sipri.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Impact of Military Artificial Intelligence on Nuclear Escalation Risk &#124; SIPRI...</span></span></span>
